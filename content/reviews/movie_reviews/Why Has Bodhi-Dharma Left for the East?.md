Why Has Bodhi-Dharma Left for the East? is a film directed, written, and produced by Bang Yong-kyun. It is certainly a lesser known movie in 2025, having only around 1,500 ratings on Letterboxd. But, it is also known for being the one of the first South Korean movies to receive a theatrical release in the United States. This movie was created at a time before Korea had a substantial film canon, and in many facets is a movie antithetical to monetary success: it is uncompromising, slow, heavily spiritual, and reflective. This movie is in cohort with directors like Tarkovsky in its philosophical and spiritual spearheads, but in many ways this movie is just as inaccessible (and in some manners even more so.) This movie lacks a clear narrative, and can be thought to be more like a Koan (a paradoxical riddle intrinsic to the Zen tradition that challenges our logical thinking and can induce spontaneous enlightenment or clarity) than a movie. But, if one is able to let go of their intuitions about how a movie ought to be and dedicate the intention espoused in the film, what you receive is perhaps one of the most important experiences you will have in your life.

The story of Why Has Bodhi-Dharma Left for the East? follows three individuals living at a Buddhist monastery in the Korean mountains, Hae-jin (a young monk), Ki-bong (an adult monk), and Hye-gok (an elderly monk). The plot is rather mundane, but this movie's use of Koans and long nature takes allows a certain profundity in even the commonplace. Though these three are separate individuals, in many ways these people represent the evolution of Zen in man, or even more broadly the seasons of the lost soul’s life. Hae-jin is constantly up to mischief and other shenanigans, and rubs up against the most significant markers in a person’s early life: death, hatred, and fear. Ki-bong has already faced these in some capacity, leaving home in the city to attain enlightenment in the mountains. Hye-gok is constantly dealing with his own mortality and the significance of his own death. In many ways he is the coming-into-being of the Zen practice, in which he recognizes the transcendence of his corporeal body. Each of these characters pose and face various koans or paradoxes in their existence.

This Koan, or cinematic experience, is incredibly atmospheric, and Bae Yong-kyun is careful in not giving the viewer any answers. While Bae Yong-kyun interrogates you and your logical baseline with the Koan, much space is left for the viewer to sink into answering these or in allowing them to flower and take root within your mind. There’s something about the slow cinema elements and the constant long, silent takes which carve out this very tranquil headspace. As someone who has training in Vipassana (mindfulness) tradition and teachings in Zen, this movie induces a mindfulness in the viewer that echoes the texture of these meditative experiences. I have never experienced a movie (besides maybe Stalker or Nostalghia, although not nearly to the same extent) which is able to create the perfect headspace for this unfettered mindfulness -- it is a movement which is quite unparalleled, and if approached without frivolity, it is like a mini-meditation retreat.

There were many moments where I would catch myself thinking during these long slides, but the sandwiching of Zen tenets and allusions to our contracting mind made these thoughts drift away and return to what was right in front of me.

“When the moon in your mind waxes beneath the water, where does the master of my being go?”

Who is the one watching this movie? The master has disappeared as film or life resumes. It is easy to identify with one of the particular characters, but it can be seen that these monks are representative of a greater human cycle. I always find myself returning to thoughts of my innocence lost, and when I have experienced the little deaths in youth which send me into flux. I have wandered many years, fearing that I am avoiding the tribulations I am fated to face, only to realize I had been coming into who I needed to be. I have found myself preoccupied with thoughts of death and of where I shall go afterwards. I have died many times, I have become the dust of the world and lighted the way. I am trash, only to be swept away by the winding torrent and stream of life. Though these experiences of these monks are typically associated with their ages, the process of life is of eternal change, and of coming and returning. 

“Was his leaving an abandonment of the world? No, his leaving home was simply a course of eternal returning.” 

We are constantly leaving, we are leaving the world “because in the world, there is no peace or freedom of the heart.” In invoking the I we leave the world and have filled ourselves with the feeling of self. Through the erasure of our self in the face of these three mosaics, we see that we have already lived the life of Hae-jin and Ki-bong, and Hye-gok -- we are constantly returning and abandoning these Koans, growing and receding, waxing and waning.

The final Koan that Hye-gok gives to Ki-bong is through his task in placing him in a coffin and burning his remains. The task is monotonous and painful, and Ki-bong sits and stares at the fire, watching as Hye-gok’s body is released into the atmosphere, melted into dust and ash. He moves through the rubble and finds Hye-gok’s remains, his bones. He crushes them, and begins to spread the ashes throughout the woods. Hye-gok is gone, but he still remains. He is everywhere: he is in the water we drink, he is dispersed through the water, and hangs in the air. He is a part of you. As you have experienced his suffering, and laughter, him shedding his body, you are deteriorating and returning to nature yourself. The light on the screen, capturing the image of Hye-gok energizes you and your eyes and changes you, and the three monks on the screen, the world that they are continually departing and returning to is the same world you leave and come home to. Why Has Bodhi-Dharma Left For The East is not a movie, and only partially a Koan; it is a return to where we are to be and nourishment for the eternal road ahead.
