This is going to be less of a blog and more of a filler for the next post. I do not have enough ideas for the next blog post, but I have enough energy to write more. So, I figured I would take a slight turn.

Up until my High School education, I rarely, if ever, thought about my consumption and waste disposal patterns — it, like many features of adolescent life, has its responsibility transferred directly and entirely to one's parents, absolving many young adults at a point in life where they probably have enough consciousness to be accountable to take their own trash out. I believe the first time I ever started thinking about my consumption patterns were when I entered college, when I had the first semblance of independence and the largest extent of freedom I have ever had; independent freedom, especially in one’s young adult life, gives one the ability to fall in similar measure with flourish. Recycling and responsible consumption habits, then, are sort of special: one does not tangibly benefit from recycling in a material way, but indirectly, from the intentional sacrificing of one’s freedom for some perceived “greater good”. Given the scale on which waste accumulates, one might not even see purported benefits in their lifetime, notwithstanding the fact that recycling is not something where one gains, but rather, a force which staves: it is a preservation of what is now, from decay.

Summing up: in 23 years of my life, I have never had to face anything resembling responsibility for my consumption habits beyond the calorie expenditure or bodily harm that comes in hauling my junk to the trash. The only incident I have had in this capacity is having a territorial dispute about whose trash belongs where, a distinctly American conflict.

In Lubbock, Texas, there is no recycling: it is a one bin system. Previous facilities that were open have been closed down; open facilities are in disparate locations, far away from most locations. Recycling is treated as something that is optional, and something which has a nontrivial amount of friction in order to actually “do.”  The most effective way to recycle a television in Lubbock, or really any place in Southeast New Mexico and West Texas is to take it to a smash room where the emissions are anger, where our TV’s destruction date is simply pushed off until later when it is thrown away again. Half a gallon of gas in order to recycle my plastic bags, and you would not be mistaken in doubting the efficiency of these recycling plants. For those who have taken a class in sustainbility analysis, it is common knowledge that the rate at which we actually recycle things in America is incredibly low. Somewhere between 15-20% of plastics in America are actually reused (which is a very generous estimate). In large part, this is brought about by apathy. But, some of this is also due to ignorance on what materials are actually recyclable and what conditions allow recycling, but I believe this is less to do with user error and more to do with mass public misinformation. 

Though, where credit is due, places like Lubbock are courageous enough to tell you that things are not going to be recycled. Many other entities governing trash give false impressions of the rate at which objects are recycled. Even waste management entities in Japan are somewhat guilty of this in posting their recycling numbers. The Plastic Waste Management Institute claimed that Japan’s recycling rate was 81% in 2021, but this is misleading, as 62% of this recycling was “thermal recycling,” which means they burned the plastic, releasing green house gases and carciongens at the source. Though there are measures to prevent this from seeping into the atmosphere, the picture is not so clear overall. Many European countries report high recycling rates as well, but the realities behind it can be quite stark: shipping waste overseas to countries which have been historically exploited by western powers, displacing of indigenous populations and deforestation to produce sustainable materials such as lithium, lack of initiatives to drop overall consumption, these few strokes are enough to illustrate a less optimistic pictures than one would hope to view.

Regardless, Japan is making much more of an effort than Lubbock, Texas. Japan remains as the fourth largest economy in the world, but has substantially smaller green house gas emissions per capita and waste production than its contemporaries (aside from Germany, perhaps.) Its approach to the problem is something which could perhaps only work in Japan itself, but which I experienced the influence of almost immediately. The most effective tool for reducing my waste thus far has been in the sheer pain in dealing with the trash system here. Japanese trash comes in four types: burnables, non-burnables in plastic, appliances with glass and ceramic, and bottles. The actual effectiveness of this sorting mechanism is not the point. You could arbitrarily decided to sort the items in terms of circles and squares, or in terms of dinosaurs and giraffes, it makes no difference: the point is that you have to actively think about what receptical you put your trash in. Who is to enforce this? Well, the garbage men do not even check the trash that they chuck in the car here. It seems that the Japanese government is aware of its own people’s capacity for social hawking and inability to mind their own business. This pressure, coupled with the sheer inconvenience of the trash system forces one to adopt a mindset which is deeply Eastern in nature: reduce your consumption habits, and you need not have to deal with the trash; address the root of the illness, and you need not worry about being sick.

Being an ALT, I arrived in Japan receiving many items from my predecessor, which was a major relief. I need not worry about having enough; but I had a new worry in that I actually had too many things. I pray to God that my predecessor never finds this post, or that he lost interest after the 5 paragraphs of dense prose over trash. I think part of why I wrote this post was to misdirect him. He left a futon here — actually, it was two futons that were stacked on top of each other and it formed what initially seemed like a couch. The first two days of being in my town were immensely stressful, and in large part because of this infernal thing. I was terrified of letting any of my clothes come into contact with it, let alone my body. The structural integrity of this futon couch thing infuriated me more than the trash system itself: anytime I tried to sit on it, there was an immediate danger of it tipping over. I also felt as though it were going to slide out from under me at any moment. I think within 48 hours, the firmest resolution I came to was not to get back to the gym, or to eat enough calories to live, but to get rid of this filthy creature.

I resolved to go to Ikea to get a new couch, and brought my teacher along with me. He asked me why I wanted to go to Ikea, and I told him about this futon. Apparently, he actually knew about this thing; actually, the whole staff room knew about this futon because my predecessor had told everyone about it. He had been boasting about the sheer geniusry in merging the two futons into a cohesive couch, and that he was incredibly excited for me to sit on it and to enjoy comfort with it. As one grows closer with those we love, we learn enough secrets (sprinkled perhaps, withs ome rhetoric) to ruin our friend’s life. My teacher could easily threaten me with the prospect of telling my predecessor about how I treated this couch and it would encourage me to do anything under his bidding. 

The oversized garbage remains a completely different category from the other previously mentioned garbages, and a special one too: one must call ahead or fill out an online application in order to actually dispose of this trash. It also is rather strange in that it is a database system where you have to sort through and add the trash disposal to a cart, so I had to purchase 4 futons to dispose of. Incidentally too, this took around 4 hours because of the fact that all of the confirm buttons were hanging off of the screen. In order for you to proceed, one had to scroll all the way down, and just barely make out the green corner of a proceed button. This felt completely impossible in Japanese, and you had to know exactly which trash station you were located by in order to do it. This story also has not seen its end: you have to book it in advance around 8 days before, so as this is being written the futon looms in the middle of my apartment, one portion in the hallway leading out of my house, and the other in my bathroom. 

In America, I would NEVER have had a second thought about throwing out this disgusting wretch of a creation, but in Japan, there have been several times where I have actively thought about how I could have made the futon work — maybe simply clean it, or just use a different base board. All of this was thought in order to avoid the annoyance of having to shuffle through my apartment and shifting my leaning futon in order to put my shoes on or to leave my apartment. The dopamine I thought I would get from finishing building my ikea couch immediately metabolized into dynorphin at the thought of having to dispose of the other monster. This system made me think more deeply about my consumption practices, though granted, a big part of this system’s effectiveness is Japan’s more collective and socially aware attitudes; whereas America seems to adopt the “mind your own business” approach.

I believe the American government would benefit more from having a trash system which is entirely in Mandarin than in English (simply by merit of the fact that you have to actually spend time sorting it) than developing more efficient systems or recycling methods. Even if you swapped the two: actually, recyclables have to be thrown away, and thrown away objects had to be recycled, it would force you to do a double take, and shock you into waking up from the recurring sleepless dream of walking to and fro the dumpster throwing your junk away. Simply by thinking about your consumption patterns, you eliminate the very need for recycling or trash sorting in the first place. That being said, I still bought the Ikea couch.
