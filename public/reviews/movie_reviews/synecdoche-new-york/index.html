Synecdoche, New York

“I know what to do with this play now. I have an idea. I think…”

Synecdoche, NY by Charlie Kauffman is one of my favorite movies all time and maybe one of the greatest movies ever made. Synecdoche, NY is significantly different in tone than movies like Being John Malkovich or Adaptation, yet still incorporates the zaniness and post-Modern flair that makes Kauffman’s work so unique. This is the opus of Kauffman, and one that is very personal to him. It reminds me of what Steinbeck said regarding the creation of his East of Eden:

“I think everything else I have written has been, in a sense, practice for this (East of Eden).”

Kauffman’s full technical prowess is on display: burning houses, gigantic mega-complexes of buildings and rooms recursively constructed in an Aleph-null cascades of our reality, and writing so capricious it can make you yell and cry in the same breath. This movie has probably made me feel the widest range of human emotion out of any I have ever watched. I have never had an experience where I have gone from hysterically laughing to crying, to feeling so angry yet quick to forgive the sheer stupidity and entitlement of our main character: lending credence to the broader human picture that Kauffman is trying to create.

I have mentioned in my previous reviews that there are certain morphisms between the literary and movie genre; I would classify this movie as being a part of some kind of “epic” tradition. It is a movie that could certainly be considered an everything movie, and one that goes about it in a truly unique way. It is far different from Yi Yi and Stalker, in a way that is completely unapologetic and supremely honest. This might turn off some viewers, but if one can forgive this vulnerability, Kauffman might afford you some of the most empathetic and impactful pieces of media you will ever consume.

There are many ways these two go about this, but I find that Caden’s descent to death, his constant hypochondria, and his constant desire to have someone guide him through his life is the through-line which allows this plot to unfold. Hoffman gives a stellar performance, and affords the perfect character for a movie about life: he is a man who is eternally ungrateful. He has two opportunities to raise children, have a family, is allowed to create exactly what he wants for a living, success, money, every possible thing that a person could constitute as the components to a good life, and he is miserably down-trodden. He is constantly thinking of his own death, his own futility, and is constantly being crushed by the weight of his responsibilities and existence, which is certainly not a unique experience. Many people are trying to find the way to live their life best – to finally find that little nugget of information which will forever change our lives and finally make us happy. It is in this play that Caden hopes to find this information, he hopes to find it in death.

“I will be dying and so will you, and so will everyone here. That’s what I want to explore. We’re all hurtling towards death, yet here we are for the moment, alive. Each of us knowing we’re going to die, each of us secretly believing we won’t.”

Strangely enough though, as his body begins to deteriorate, so does Caden’s own sense of self. It seems as though Caden has already died before his physical death has even arrived. Death in Kauffman’s universe is something that is met not in one moment at the end, but is found en-masse, every second in the collective unconscious. His constant occupation with sickness and heartbreak has forced him to imagine a world where he is no longer alive: he tries to live in everyone else’s life through his play, maybe to make some sense out of the situation. The idea that describes Caden most is being lost: he is constantly looking to others (particularly, Hazel) to tell him how to live life correctly, how to make the proper decision where he can finally be happy, and maybe not cry so much before sex. Simultaneously, this immense sense of selflessness is highly self-motivated, creating a major contrast between a collectivist backdrop being crunched down to the individual level. The message is contradictory: Caden victimizes himself; he is constantly trying to make sense of his own situation, yet is impotent to actually proceed. He acts very selfishly as well, he constantly pursues Hazel and is completely emotionally unavailable to her when it matters most, he marries Madeleine even though he is still completely in love with Hazel, and never truly gets over her.

“Hazel, you’ve been a part of me forever. Don’t you know that? I breathe your name in every exhalation.”

Caden’s own identity is not clear, he has breathed Hazel’s name in every breath. He changes and rebels against Sam’s deterministic caricatures of him, he finds solace in being Ellen, Adele’s housewife. Caden’s constant observation of other people, his very creation of his world with over 13 million people is a creation that represents his broader disintegration of self. The meta-physic of this universe is where the “self” either doesn’t exist, or every self that does exist is compounded within each body. It strangely resembles the world view of Erwin Schrodinger, albeit with an idealist metaphysical slant:

“What is it that has called you so suddenly out of nothingness to enjoy for a brief while a spectacle which remains quite indifferent to you? The conditions for your existence are almost as old as the rocks. For thousands of years men have striven and suffered and begotten and women have brought forth in pain. A hundred years ago, perhaps, another man sat on this spot; like you he gazed with awe and yearning in his heart at the dying light on the glaciers. Like you he was begotten of man and born of woman. He felt pain and brief joy as you do. Was he someone else? Was it not you yourself? What is this Self of yours? What was the necessary condition for making the thing conceived this time into you, just you and not someone else? What clearly intelligible sczentific meaning can this ‘someone else’ really have? If she who is now your mother had cohabited with someone else and had a son by him, and your father had done likewise, would you have come to be? Or were you living in them, and in your father’s father. ..thousands of years ago? And even if this is so, why are you not your brother, why is your brother not you, why are you not one of your distant cousins? What justifies you in obstinately discovering this difference— the difference between you and someone else—when objectively what is there is the same?” (My View of the World, pg. 20)

“…If you analyse it closely you will, I think, find that it is just a little bit more than a collection of single data (experiences and memories), namely the canvas upon which they are collected. And you will, on close introspection, find that what you really mean by ‘I’ is that ground-stuff upon which they are collected. You may come to a distant country, lose sight of all your friends, may all but forget them; you acquire new friends, you share life with them as intensely as you ever did with your old ones. Less and less important will become the fact that, while living your new life, you still recollect the old one. ‘The youth that was I’, you may come to speak of him in the third person, indeed the protagonist of the novel you are reading is probably nearer to your heart, certainly more intensely alive and better known to you. Yet there has been no intermediate break, no death. And even if a skilled hypnotist succeeded in blotting out entirely all your earlier reminiscences, you would not find that he had killed you. In no case is there a loss of personal existence to deplore.” (What is life?, pg.90)

Caden, like many people in the world, wishes to take a step back from the person he has penned himself to be his entire life. The culminating moment of this is when Sam commits suicide:

“I’ve watched you forever, Caden, but you’ve never really looked at anyone other than yourself. So watch me. Watch my heart break. Watch me jump. Watch me learn that after death there’s nothing. There’s no more watching. There’s no more following. No love. Say goodbye to Hazel for me. And say it to yourself, too. None of us has much time.”

Sam’s death is a sort of death of himself, and he experiences many smaller deaths throughout the film. These internal contradictions produce a work which is immensely absurd, and is further potentiated by some downright ridiculous scenes. Olive, Caden’s long-lost daughter who is now a pole-dancer, absolutely inked up, accuses him of being gay, and consequently an affair with his lover being the reason that their family fell apart. The moment is genuinely depressing, but immensely hilarious as well. Caden apologizes for something that he did not really do. He feels guilty for something ostensibly false, and is willing to apologize for actions he did not believe himself to take. However, in tandem with Caden’s destabilizing sense of self, this apologetic moment is less so about his theoretical gay lover, and the fact that he is in some sense an inherently guilty creature.He is not apologizing for being gay (when he isn’t even), he is apologizing for the bare fact of being alive, for impotently suffering, and being broken. In some sense, it might be Caden filling in for the role of someone else having an affair, yet also simultaneously being responsible for it as well. In Kauffman’s universe, it is almost as if we ought to apologize for things that are not apparently our fault, because everything is so interconnected that we might be more responsible for other people’s sufferings than they themselves.

“Everything is more complicated than you think. You only see a tenth of what is true. There are a million little strings attached to every choice you make; you can destroy your life every time you choose. But maybe you won’t know for twenty years. And you may never ever trace it to its source. And you only get one chance to play it out. Just try and figure out your own divorce. And they say there is no fate, but there is: it’s what you create…”

I find this reality strangely compelling, yet the most real. Everything is within our control, yet everything is out of it. Things are completely our fault, yet also no one’s fault. We are simply on full launch to the grave. After some truly devastating portions of the movie, he takes a break from the play and becomes Millicent for a while. He experiences all of her sorrows, all of her hopes and dreams, and how they fail to come about. Millicent gives an ear-piece to Caden and he can finally live. He is no longer so listless in his exploration of what the true life is: he finally is given the key. Millicent tells him exactly how to live as Ellen. And here are the instructions, now that he has been given the key to live:

“What was once before you - an exciting, mysterious future - is now behind you. Lived; understood; disappointing. You realize you are not special. You have struggled into existence, and are now slipping silently out of it. This is everyone’s experience. Every single one. The specifics hardly matter. Everyone’s everyone. So you are Adele, Hazel, Claire, Olive. You are Ellen. All her meager sadnesses are yours; all her loneliness; the gray, straw-like hair; her red raw hands. It’s yours. It is time for you to understand this…As the people who adore you stop adoring you; as they die; as they move on; as you shed them; as you shed your beauty; your youth; as the world forgets you; as you recognize your transience; as you begin to lose your characteristics one by one; as you learn there is no-one watching you, and there never was, you think only about driving - not coming from any place; not arriving any place. Just driving, counting off time. Now you are here, at 7:43. Now you are here, at 7:44. Now you are..gone.”

He is instructed to do the one thing we will all have to do: die. After all, the end was built into the beginning.
