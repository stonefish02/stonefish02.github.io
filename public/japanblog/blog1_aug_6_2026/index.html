Moby Dick is a Pink Gilded Breakfast Ticket (Blog 1)

So this will be the first blog entry. It feels a little bit rushed, but for the time being, it’s really just about getting the ball rolling, and getting into the habit of making semi-frequent posts. That being said, I am not so certain if I will make it a weekly endeavor. Maybe it will become an event that I post when I feel like a sufficient amount happens, interspersed with the occasional essay or thought to keep things fresh. This blog is here to give people my experience as a teacher in Hyogo-ken, but more broadly as an American living in Japan. Whether this move was an escape, or me running along the path I need to traverse is not fully clear to me at this point; I think that line is rather thin. This blog is apart of a broader mindfulness program on my part, and as such, some of the posts might be a little dense, perhaps even terse at times. But, for those who are not my direct friends and family, I can also guarantee you that it will be worth reading outside of seeing what is happening to me. I am hoping to develop more of a knack for writing non-fiction. Where this will lead me in my future career endeavors is yet to be fully realized, but many good things turn out to be materially fruitless.

So I am not exactly sure what the tone of this will be: there will be anecdotes, essays, and reflections. I don’t want to attempt to unfold my philosophy, or what my experiences will look like right now, because it will simultaneously be evolving and changing as I go out into the world. I hope only to experience the difficult and embarassing, perhaps even the magical and sublime, which will in turn hopefully be the most useful. I also can not really guarantee that this will maintain the same tone throughout, as polyphonic as the mind may be, so this blog will be as well; I will always attempt to be mostly authentic and unblunted in my experience, insofar as I hopefully do not believe in my own lies throughout this journey.

Anyways, that’s not to say that I will say anything unkind or controversial, more my goal is to not be afraid of being vulnerable, and also to be unfettered in my opinion of what is occurring hopefully tinted with the least bias possible. I would like to call this a symphony, but I think it is more similar to a shogazer who plays his electric cello with a screwdriver, or any other amount of household objects at his disposal. I want to make this blog academic, that is, maybe interspersed with a few footnotes or citations every now and then, but not so academic and dry that my voice is lost masquerading as word made flesh. Really, this prelude is just an excuse for me to paste in quotes or articles I have found that have come to funnel my current mind-stream, blended with my current experience in Japan, so there also might be a little scientology here as well. But also, this blog, or really my greater journaling and writing project is in search of my writing voice, and what the character of it is. Patience is appreciated, and I think it will be rewarded.

I have spent most of my time in the United States, and have never left the country. Most of my life I have engaged in hobbies and activities that are typical of the introvert type, and there is a component of me that does feel somewhat dissatisfied with this. I have read Sartre’s Nausea, so I realize that Roquentin might be rolling in his literary grave in me writing this. Of course, someone could simply say that this is a perfectly acceptable way to live one’s life, and with enough CBT and zen one could accept this, but for me, I seem to have grown attached to this idea that I want to have a few adventures before I decide to turn in and drag myself by the academic stone, and that I will benefit from the experience if I do so.

Though I would like to think myself well-educated and free from ignorance, creating an image in your head of what will happen and your response to it is far different from experiencing it direct-hand, and my experience over the first few days in Japan certainly resonated previous anecdotes I have read, but also illustrated my sheer helplessness in responding effectively. Quite frankly, I have never felt more foolish in my life living in Japan. I had some working understanding of knowledge of Japan and its culture, I thought. But, beyond watching anime, listening to the occasional Japanese music or reading a novel, sprinkled with a little google every now and then, this country remains one of my major artistic blind spots. In many facets its reputation certainly precedes itself, and seems to be the physical escape of choice for many Americans in my generation. I have had around 2 months of study in Japanese before this journey, which in the grand scheme of a language is like cosmic inflation starting in the universe: nothing in time scale, but perhaps one of the most important predeterminers of my trajectory.

It seems that a lot of people in my generation are interested in vising this place because of a certain mystique, and I can not completely absolve myself of this either. Japan has this notion of being a mythical ant-colony blooming with technological and artistic innovation; Neon lights in Tokyo have their slanting particles cast all the way in America, and here I am on riding on the teaching ray over. In a lot of ways, Japan seems to be living about 700 years in the future, but also seems to be rather slow in other areas. In this way, Japan experiences a great pinch: there is one hand, the hand of tradition pulling one way, and another, equally strong hand pulling in the reverse. After enough time under tension, it seems that the society has grown quite taut, and in some ways its colloquial problem holes are starting to grow larger, especially with recent economic troubles and pressure from outside and inside influences.

After spending a little bit of time in Japan, I have come to think of it as a colloquial “great filter,” that is, though Japan has been subject to foreign involvement, cultural exchange, and imperialism, there still remains a core Japanese identity which has remained unchanged. Indeed, Japan is also immensely pragmatic, but suffers from a lot of the same problems that numerical models in mathematics do: a lot of it is built upon old reposotories and software from the bone ages, and everything is built on top of. Sometimes, there is more of a market for making an already existing software package work, rather than throwing out the ideas all together. This results in a sort of resistence to change, but levels of hyper-innovation when outside influence is actually accepted. Japan in many ways is open to change more full-heartedly than any other nation, so long as it is in keeping with the values and traditions of Japan. This great paradox illuminates a highly structured society, with highly motivated citizens, and rich plethoras of ideas and artistic expression. A future idea I would like to explore follows what Plato supposes in his republic: every nation sees its mirror inside of the citizens, and even its language.

But, more concretely, even the most commonplace tasks or places are highly structured, and thus highly resistant to special or pathological cases, so much so that the Gaijin (foreigner) can be the colloquial iceberg in a number of cases. There are many levels of Japanese society which are highly bureaucratized which makes even the most commonplace tasks somewhat inconvenient (though, this is not alltogether a bad thing in certain cases, such as its sophisticated trash system). This has coined the idea of the Gaijin smash: a special move in which many foreigners can override existing power structures in Japan due to the adherance to, a greater, more powerful structure in Japanese society: 平和 (hei-wa), which can be thought of as atmospheric (perhaps even suffocating) social harmony.

My first week in Japan (well, it’s not really the first full week, but I will act like it is because it felt even longer than that) was an amazing, but truly strange experience. I simultaneously felt completely unprepared even after my orientation, but am not exactly sure what I would do to change it even if it were left to me with total power. Unfortunately, even with the presentation, people with my disposition and temperament are destined to have mishaps in travelling. I suffer from being a HSP (highly sensitive person), which is actually a real medical term, but I need no validation: all I know is that any overwhelming situation is followed by complete synaptic overload and as if someone took a sledge hammer to my nervous system, but it is more apt to say I ran into a metaphorical stalwart wall that I didn’t seem to notice.

We were briefed on what the process for entering Japan was at some kind of orientation. When I arrived at Narita airport in Tokyo, there were two QR codes we needed to have: the first one was simply one that is for entering Japan filled out online. I filled out the form but I couldn’t find the QR code. No problem: I was in the back of the line with Forrest and we figured out we could just write on the paper forms. I got my residence card no problem, though when I spoke Japanese with the attendant who was helping me, I think I have her false hope. She proceeded to say something I did not understand and I think I disapointed her because she was silent for the rest of it. Was it better to lower the bar or raise it? Anyways, I get through to the next area where our luggage is supposed to be. Now, all the while, I am the last in line and basically everyone has made it through customs aside from a few people. I ask a fellow JET to help me out, and either they do not hear me or are tired of helping me with things that seemed obvious to everyone else, and I am left….

Alone. Most of the JET attendants left, I was left thinking my last hope walking away from me was the last one. I am at a random desk trying to find an English customs form, but for some reason every language was indecypherable to me. I looked around and could not find it, and I think for a good 2-3 minutes I stood in complete silence not sure at all what to do. I thought about going to ask someone, but I was such a tired mess that no comprehensible Japanese would be able to leave my mouth, and I think to some degree I just decided to accept my fate. Luckily, two wonderful attendants come up to me and ask “大丈夫ですか？” and began helping me. With what little English they had, they actually informed me that the form I was filling out was actually in Chinese the whole time, so no wonder the Japanese felt particularly indecypherable… I had to switch some things out of my carry on and checked bag, so I sat there with everyone’s gaze like the sun cooking me and the east Japan heat battering me worse than any other climate I’ve experienced. I genuinely felt as though I was overheating and my brain was being thermal throttled like a computer, the mere act of moving objects between suitcases required Herculean strength and Einsteinian intellect. Now I am in attendance with around 6 other people, one or two call me “Bi-I-P,” and if they had sunglasses and ear pieces my ego would have inflated large enough to install a pre-selected dictator in the airport I was sauntering about in. We make it to the bus stop where we are supposed to pick up our bags. I see my bag, and thinking the end is in sight, they ask me where my bus pass is.

Bus pass. Somehow, in the midst of all of this I had also lost my bus pass that they left in a bright yellow envelope, and now I had around 15 employees around me laughing at my misfortune. They manage to hand make a new bus ticket with a copy they had, and instead of making me do it, they insisted I wait there while they handcopied my new bus ticket. There were definitely a few remarks about how foolish I was in a rather indirect way, but what was so novel about this experience for me was the fact that they helped me all the way through to the end, telling me everything was okay while also being unable to hold back their smiles. I was one small exception, and it felt like every hand was on deck in order to get me to the bus. I have never experienced this level of helpfulness in my entire life, and I spent the entire busride to the hotel in silence taking in this moment. It felt as though this plan for rescuing me was planned from the start. I can’t really imagine how this situation would have played out in America.

To some extent though, this was maybe a perfect experience to have on entering the country, because it is has made my tolerance for embarassment at least slightly higher. A lot of the tragedy in this situation comes from the fact that I was unable to communicate with the staff, or rather, the anxiety I had in thinking I would be unable to communicate with the staff. During my work’s orientation, I met a lot of wonderful people who came to Japan for very different reasons. I had two roommates in the hotel I was staying at, a man named Roch (pronounced Rock) and another man named Sam. Roch and I of course became good chaps pretty quickly. I was able to get out of the hotel a few times and walk around. Traversing Tokyo while immensely jet lagged was a sort of surreal experience: we stayed around Shinjuku station where it is completely overcrowded and something was always going on. The heat hanging in the air was less from the power of nature and more from the sheer immensity of people shuffling in to any given street. I did not have as much time to explore Tokyo as I would have liked, but the place felt truly infinite. My mind could not anticipate the absurdity of three story Wendy’s, several story panchinko parlors, and Godzilla head, and there was something terribly dreadful about the experience lurking in the background. Certainly the novelty was enough to make me feel nice in a few moments, but there is something wildly dystopian in passing through the red light district and seeing desperate people shuffling in and out of host(ess) clubs, and anime girls with blue pigtails dangling out of the window beckoning people in 애교 . I think this will beget a future essay, and I will hyperlink it here if it comes about.

Embarrassment became tinged with annoyance a few days later as I tried to obtain breakfast at the hotel we were staying at. The orientation itself will be explored in another essay. One of the people I had befriended the previous evening (who looks exactly like my friend Kareem) mentions wanting to go get breakfast, so I accompany him. We were told the previous evening by our group coordinators that breakfast would be open at 5 o’clock in the morning. We were supposed to meet up alltogether at 6:35, 30 minutes before the bus departed. It is around 5:15 o’clock when I first encounter my stalwart friend, and we resolve to go to the 5th floor where breakfast was supposed to be open at 5 o’clock. We arrive in the room, and everything is set up, but no food is being unearthed. We make a vague inquisition about breakfast, and they say it will not be open until 6:30. We are of course, a little confused, but walking out we encounter some other JETs who say that they were told breakfast would be held on the second floor. As my friend and I are walking along, there are two girls who are walking to the second floor, and one of them asks us if we have our breakfast tickets. Tickets? My friend and I are confused, and she pulls out a pretty pink ticket, gilded with JET Programme cascading across the center. We decide to go down anyways, and ask the attendant at his host table if we can enter.

This man, who will soon become my lifelong enemy, tells us to go up to guest services, so we go up the stairs and arrive there; the official customer guest man acknowledges our problem, disappears for 5 minutes, then returns telling us that the breakfast is actually on the 4th floor. We, of course, go up to the 4th floor and there is no such breakfast. We suppose that it is actually the 5th floor he meant, so we go up and explain our case to the people on the 5th floor, and without the least strain or dent, the attendants tell us it is open at 6:30.

So, I go back downstairs to the 2nd floor and try to explain my situation to the attendant. In retrospect, I think I should have made a better movement to try and communicate with Japanese, but after JET lag and three days of useless training and early breakfasts, any amount of complex thought was not occuring in my brain. By the end of the conversation, after explaining the arduous loops I had run myself in, he simply begins saying in capital letters NO. NO YOU CAN NOT. NO TICKET. As I walk away, him chuckling to his coworker likely about the conversation which just unfolded, I encounter another group of JETs who were told that breakfast is on the second floor. They, this time, have a supervisor on speed dial who will explain the situation. The sallowed cheeks of the host sink as he sees another group of hungry gaijin looking for breakfast, as he sees my toes lengthen into my legs lengthen into my curly hair and green tie, his eyes roll back and in its wake is left a scream mask face of anger. He is immediately on the offensive, and says NO. NO YOU CAN NOT. NO TICKET. Even after every possible way of trying to avoid the ticket, he remained completely obstinate. I respect my enemy as much as I detest him for his rigidity. He did not make any effort to try and talk to people about what was going on, the second floor breakfast was his kingdom and I was not allowed to enter. Afterwards I settle for konbini breakfast at the 7/11 on the basement floor. There is a 7/11 everywhere in Tokyo, and its breakfast was okay. It was a weak consolation for this breakfast, but one nonetheless.

What was so strange about these two experiences is that I was a sufferer in the same breath that I was its beneficiary, yet both situations I felt completely helpless. Stabbed by a finnish knife, I also managed to meet my wife, and our engagement ring was a carabiner.

So titrating: what did I learn? I’m not really sure thus far. I think I have come to respect a certain moral obstinance from the Japanese breakfast nazi, and realize that I have much to learn as I proceed in my journey. まだまだです。
