Gaman (我慢）

Trial by fire. There is hardship. A lot of it. The most special contributor to this hardship is the loneliness that I feel here in Japan. One part is cultural, another section is life in the big city. I do not want to lend too much information regarding my location, because the people who ought to know where I am already know, and anyone who doesn’t is probably not in an immediacy that would mentally condone me sharing that information. But, I live in one of the large metropolitan areas in Japan, a swath of land which is far larger than any place I have been to in my entire life, and one which encodes its own adjustment. There are certain features of big city life which are nice and quite convenient. Postal services deliver within 1 or 2 days, there are multiple conbini on each block, there is no shortage of things to do in the realm of entertainment; yet there is an omnipresent and looming rigidity of this society which feels impossible to break through. I explored this a little bit in my comments on the Japanese waste disposal system, but another one of its symptoms is that it feels like it is incredibly difficult to connect with people here, even beyond just a language barrier.

Dealing with this loneliness itself has also been kind of shocking. When I first moved to Lubbock, in a lot of ways I was completely starting from scratch. I have been lonely many times before, and even in the most familiar places. But, living in Japan has revealed some very nasty things about my own personality. For one: my attachment to solitude and self-studying has made me incredibly selfish. Moreover, it has shown how defensive of a human being I am, or how I have a major tendency to overexplain things. I was told this by other people before, but it has only been until now that I realize how deeply this has affected me. I think this warrants another personal essay in and of itself: being a good talker simultaneously gets you out of very terrible rhetorical situations, but can also prevent you from taking responsibility from your actions.

In this vein, being unable to communicate how you truly feel to people, or maybe, more cynically, to explain away any mistakes or ignorance can make things very difficult. Beyond my experience at orientation, I have had an exceeding amount of moments where I have made myself look like a complete fool. Being rather honest with myself as well, this has sometimes made me dread going to do even the most simple of tasks. Finding friends has not been super easy, especially because I no longer have school to force me to be around other people, not to mention, my lifestyle is somewhat solitary as it is. Even though I had very grandiose ideas about how I would be spending my time in Japan, I have come to institute something that in large part resembles the routine I had when I was in Lubbock. Making a spontaneous charge to be a spontaneous traveller and “liver of life” is not so simple.

There are two different modes by which I have tried to circumvent this loneliness: one is by studying Japanese harder, convincing myself that solace is found in my eventual ability to communicate with people. Yet, there have been many moments where people in my organization have posted meet up events and I did not go, or moments I could have struck conversations with people. I frame this as a problem of not trying to get stuck in the “foreigner bubble,” yet my awareness is developed enough to jetty-jack this idea, and plant the sword of the “middle way” in the ground — so it seems to me that this has unveiled a certain avoidant tendency in dealing with my loneliness, and there even seems to be a masochistic component to it. There have been moments where I have felt overwhelmed by people, no doubt made spikier by a sudden evaporation of social ease, a challenge which I knew (and wanted) to face.

Additionally, an unhealthy idea I have been coming to grips with relates to the notion of the tortured artist. The masochistic component of my loneliness comes in perpetuating it because I find that I have my best ideas when I am alone. Even though I know this is not good for one’s mental health, there is the optimization obsessed part of me which wants to teeter that edge in a way which is most conducive to my creativity and flourishing of my art. The premise of this underlying argument is one which is quite strange to me. There is the one part of me which understands how doing things like going to the gym, or even meditating is generally good for how you feel, but in equal measure there is a competing vanity which uses my body as the fuel to fire.

Even without this though, there have been times recently where I am walking down a busy road and I feel as though I am the loneliest person there has ever been, which makes me feel like a character from a Voltaire novel. In that moment, I think that if my thoughts were transmitted through an aether wire to the approaching citizen, they would immediately ask me if I was okay and we would get to talking: they would praise me for my resilience, and tell me I’ve lived a hard life. Though this may be true factually, it is a little strange when I consider how often this news bit is ran through my head. How often this fiber is sewn into the muscle of each action is only now becoming apparent to me. At the surface level, there seems to be an immense apathy to the sufferings of other people in this country, and it can be seen in just walking around here. One of the strangest things I have seen here are elderly people who are hobbling around in Japanese summers at 1 mile per hour, yet bearing their strife with the utmost dignity; disabled people proceeding through their day at rates faster than most middle aged Americans; students undergoing slews of entrance exams, workers decimated by 60-70 hour work weeks; an unending acceptance of natural disasters and setbacks which would leave most people without a desire for life. That is not to say people do not experience ignominious, stark defeat and others do not, but it seems that the Japanese people have developed a cultural framework that does not solve, but simply bears these problems, and actually expresses it as a certain form of pride. I posit that a reason why Japanese life expectancy (and also suicide rate) is so high is not because of mere diet, but it is something that many of their citizens have ingrained down to their bones and heart:

我慢 (Gaman) is a Japanese word which does not have an exact translation to English, but can be roughly approximated as a sort of patience in dealing with hardship. It intersects with resilience, but it is not a passive resignation — it is in bearing this hardship tempered with endless acceptance. It is a virtue highly aware of itself, yet deeply ignorant. It is a virtue which strikes its own use as a means to vain ends. It is a virtue which I simultaneously worship, yet also see immense harm in when not in unison with the other virtues. Gaman’s development, and perhaps even necessity is predicated on a deep history founded upon rigid, violent social structures, and a sort of helplessness in defeating these power structures. From the American perspective, passivity and resilience know their distinction in its end: resilience only sees its affirmation in the pinnacle of justice. Yet Japanese Gaman needs no end. In fact, much of Gaman professes to be ultimately fruitless and fatalistic, and it may perhaps be even more beautiful because of its utter uselessness, in the same way a Christian may have their faith shaken by Hans Hoblein the Younger’s “The Body of the Dead Christ in the Tomb,” the Japanese philosopher may waver with incredulity, as Thomas does in Caravaggio’s depiction of the risen Christ.

The seemingly immortal Japanese is parasytically prolonged by the system which drains its very life force, having crushed bones and souls into conformity, grinded together gears clacking in unison in the great hum of national and communal unity. Though the Japanese Gaman is simultaneously beautiful, it is also at times contradictory and even self-imposed. Gaman in a certain way is a blindness, an a-priori masochism people are willing to suffer for actualization. Gaman to me reads like the sort of obsession one finds in kicking a rock with your right foot for a mile, solving a mathematical proof which you can not seem to crack, or those generally endless, random, and necessarily useless objects which suddenly attract our entire attention and could but be the only universe we would (seemingly) choose to live in. It is certainly Sisyphean in image. It is not a virtue worth dying for, because death would be its renunciation, but one which many people who live in Japanese society have renounced in those dread forests. Though, the end more optimistically might be alikened to those post-apocalyptic images we see of vegetation and thrush overrunning concrete landscapes, modern society laying like artifacts swept away by the sands of time, humanity being a sort of vegetation speckled in the midst of a slew of mechanical mountains.

The point is that I have had to develop a bit of Gaman myself, and maybe also not in the most productive fashion possible. In travelling to a new place, there seems to be this notion that if approached with the correct intent, one unlocks a new mode of meditation which is detached from one’s previous life, their home in the rear view, an objective puzzle piece master sits in the seat of consciousness where the major components of one’s past are clicked together, this puzzle being the basis for a grander mosaic. I’ve come to experience that when one is stripped of those homely comforts, it can actually bring the worst parts of ourselves to the forefront. One of those negative facets of my personality is that I spend too much time searching out information, rather than thinking about the ones I have already learned. There have been countless evenings where I have tried to understand a situation which has originated in my thought, to write a simple description and to append the suffix “Reddit” at the end in order to finally find the answer, where, to paraphrase Proust, there seems to some book in the library, some passage, in which that answer is finally found and will resolve all that search and longing.

This loneliness is tempered by the torment that I have never had an original thought. When I am reading my books, I am always simultaneously yearning and craving for output, but sometimes I stuff it down by consuming more, by eating and drinking more and ravenously devouring works and ideas. But at this moment I have come to the realization that writing and creation are not actually creating something new, but are simply just a process of remembrance. Experience anew is but that past refraction. This stream of thought is continued in Inner & Outer.
